## The artist of the Neck

High along the **[[Spine of the World]]**, where the mountains narrow toward Avalor's northern coast, lies a country sometimes called **the Neck**. Few outsiders encountered the nomadic Goliath clan that moved along its windward slopes. Those who did might have remembered a people of strength, endurance, old customs, and fierce competition. They were less likely to know what the clan reserved for its artists.

Music, sculpture, painting, dancing, and poetry all held places of honor among Mandu's people. In art, where excellence could not be settled by a race or a contest of strength, the clan's habitual rivalry gave way to appreciation. A gifted artist offered something that could not be measured against another person's achievement.

**Mandu Durbar-Marg** had such a gift. He was born into a family of modest standing: his father, **Ilaichee**, was a cook whose carefully maintained garden supplied much of the clan's food; his mother, **Staanpan**, was a wet nurse who had helped raise generations of children. Both found dignity in useful work rather than public boasting. Mandu inherited their quietness, though his attention was drawn less to the bustle of the encampment than to the water visible below.

## Color where the mountain meets the sea

The clan followed the coastward, windward side of the Neck, where the sea gentled the harsh mountain air enough for herbs to flourish and goats to graze. Ilaichee noticed the figures his son scratched into stone. Staanpan showed him how berries, bark, flowers, and herbs could be crushed into pigments. From those simple materials Mandu found a language of his own.

When the noise and contests of clan life grew oppressive, he slipped down to the shore. The white quartz outcroppings gave him surfaces upon which light and color could take hold. He began with the things before him—birds, breaking waves, fish, shells, and the ships that sometimes crossed the horizon. Soon he painted things he could only imagine lay beyond it.

His murals stood where weather and tide could reach them. He returned nonetheless, less concerned with possessing a finished work than with the act of making what he saw, remembered, or hoped to see.

## Kathis beneath the waves

When Mandu was **seventeen**, a Triton named **Kathis** came to speak with him at the shore. Kathis had watched the murals from the water for years. The artist who had spent so long looking out to sea discovered that someone had been looking back.

Their conversations became meetings, then a familiar ritual. Kathis described cities beneath the waves, reefs stretching like forests, and people who understood currents as Mandu's kin understood mountain trails. The Triton's occasional arrogance amused the otherwise solemn young Goliath. With Kathis, Mandu smiled more easily than he did among his own people.

Almost a year passed in this fashion. Their bond grew through curiosity and companionship, the exchange of stories, and the quiet pleasure of returning to a place where they expected to find one another.

## The cliff and the eighteenth birthday

Near Mandu's **eighteenth birthday**, Kathis brought him coral of deep purple and brilliant orange. They ground it into pigment, mixed it with seawater, and painted together along the cliffs. Afterwards, resting on a natural reef beyond the breakers, they admired what they had made.

Their hands met. Neither withdrew. They leaned together and kissed, awkwardly and tenderly, with nearly a year's unspoken feeling behind it.

A scream broke the moment. **Staanpan** stood above them on the cliff, having come in search of her son with pigments for his birthday. Among Mandu's clan, a relationship between people of different species was forbidden. Seeing him in Kathis's arms, she fled toward the encampment.

Mandu and Kathis understood what would follow. They shared one more kiss, and Kathis disappeared beneath the sea. **Mandu never saw him again.**

By the time Mandu returned, the clan had pronounced its judgment. His parents were nowhere to be found. No contest could overturn the decision, and no apology would restore his place. He was **banished upon penalty of death if he returned**.

He left the mountains and followed the coast, watching the water long after any reasonable hope of seeing Kathis again had faded. Exile had taken his home, but it had not ended his search.

## The shell and the Marid

On Mandu's **first night alone**, he found a small shell pendant on the shoreline. Its inner surface bore carved symbols resembling Triton glyphs Kathis had shown him, though their forms seemed much older.

Rain began to fall, and a **Marid** appeared. Its shape shifted like the sea: sometimes forceful and masculine, sometimes serene and feminine, with neither appearance more essential than the other.

The being offered no easy reunion. It could not simply summon Kathis back to Mandu. Instead it proposed the means to continue searching: an affinity with water, knowledge of Triton culture, and magic able to carry him farther than his own strength. In return, it demanded **devotion, honor, love, and service**.

Mandu had turned eighteen without a home or a future he recognized. He accepted the bargain. The Marid became a continuing presence in his travels, giving him tasks whose meanings were not always clear. The shell's older symbols and the full purposes of those tasks remain mysteries in Mandu's story.

## A world recorded in color

Years of travel changed Mandu without ending the longing that first sent him along the shore. He lived among coastal settlements, sailors, fishers, nomads, islanders, artists, and peoples whose homes lay below the waves. He learned to listen before asking questions. Others came to call him an **anthropologist**, a title he neither sought nor valued much.

What interested him was the way a people made itself known. A woven pattern, a ceremonial garment, a carved figure, a painted wall, an ornament, or the shape of a building could speak of memories and duties that ordinary conversation did not immediately reveal.

Mandu rarely collected the objects he studied. Instead, **he painted them**. His journals became portable galleries: field notes, travel records, studies of color and design, and reminders of people he had met. The Marid continued to set him tasks. Some were straightforward; others seemed to lead by symbols, rumors, or fragments of knowledge toward the search he had never abandoned.

His magic expanded what he could reach. His painting helped him remember why he had gone.

## Portraits that could not be kept

**Kathis remained at the center of Mandu's private life.** He painted his lost love again and again, sometimes keeping a portrait for months. Then a detail would trouble him: the curve of a smile, the angle of an ear, the look in his eyes. Fearful that the painted face might slowly replace the face he remembered, Mandu would destroy the work.

His murals followed another rule. Along cliffs, bridges, ruined walls, ports, and solitary shores, he left images in a style recognizable to anyone who knew his hand. They were neither maps nor messages written in ordinary words. They were signs of passage, left in the hope that the right person might one day find them.

*I was here. I am still looking. Find me.*

The work of making these images tied the young exile to the wider world. He could belong briefly to places he had never known as home, while preserving a thread back to the person he hoped was still somewhere beyond the water.

## A hunter who learned to observe

Mandu is counted among the **[[Hunters Guild]]**, the far-traveling fellowship whose refuge is the **[[Wayward Sun]]** in Avalor's **[[Dead Marshes]]**. Where many hunters are remembered for tracking monsters or enduring wilderness dangers, Mandu's established gifts lie in close observation, painting, knowledge gathered among different peoples, and the waterborne magic of his bargain.

Those abilities belong naturally beside a guild tradition that values patience, careful knowledge, and protection beyond the reach of ordinary patrols. His place among the hunters does not erase the life that brought him there. The artist from the Neck remains a traveler searching for Kathis.

## A night of laughter in Stanleyville

Long after he had left the Neck, Mandu encountered a familiar face in **[[Stanleyville]]**. **[[minor-heroes-and-allies#milos-digan|Milos Digan]]**, a childhood friend, was there with **Joy**, a pregnant Tiefling whose hand he held. Milos had departed the clan for Joy before Mandu's banishment and had not witnessed the judgment against his friend.

The three spent the night talking, drinking, and laughing. Milos recalled his hunting adventures, to Joy's considerable annoyance. Joy spoke of the quieter life she hoped they might build near **[[Stannisport]]**. With them, Mandu rediscovered a companionship he had believed banishment might have severed forever.

His clan had rejected him, but **not everyone he had known had done so**. For one evening the years of separation seemed less important than the ease with which old friends could speak to one another.

For years afterward, it was the last time Mandu had seen Milos.

## The charcoal portrait

A little more than a year later, **a letter arrived from Joy** with word that Milos was dead. Mandu had no reason then to question the report.

Mandu returned to the same inn in Stanleyville and took the same table where they had laughed together. He sat with an untouched ale in one hand and Joy's letter in the other. As the fire sank low, he reached into the cold hearth and drew out a piece of charcoal.

On the back of the letter, he began to draw Milos from memory. First came the outline of his head, then his eyes, then the small crease at the corner of his mouth that appeared just before he said something foolish. Mandu worked carefully, refusing to hurry the details.

When the portrait was finished, he laid it upon the table and remained there until morning. He watched the face of the friend he would not see again, and he wept.

For years, Mandu had destroyed portraits of Kathis for fear that paint might distort the memory of someone he hoped to find. Believing Milos lost forever, Mandu thought there could be no future meeting to correct a mistaken line. All he had that night was memory, charcoal, and the time it took to draw.

The boy who had learned to speak through color in the Neck had carried that language across the world. On that night in Stanleyville, with no pigments and no sea before him, **it was still how he remembered someone he loved**.

Later, the hunters learned that Milos had been **found alive in the Crucible as the Den Mother's champion**, after a vision of his apparent petrification and destruction had seemed to confirm his death. Mandu and Milos had not yet spoken again. The discovery did not make the letter, the night of grief, or the charcoal portrait any less real; it changed what Mandu could know about the fate of the friend he mourned.
