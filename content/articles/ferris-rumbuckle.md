## A boy with more questions than courage

In the woods around **Tumbledown**, a small gnome village near the edges of the **Feywild**, strange sounds could send a child hurrying home before dusk. They could also send **Ferris Rumbuckle** looking for their source.

Ferris was curious about almost everything and frightened of a surprising amount of it. An unfamiliar track beneath wet leaves might make him imagine the worst creature the forest could conceal. He would hesitate, consider turning back, and then realize he could not bear to leave the question unanswered.

Sometimes he came back with a discovery. Sometimes he came back running. In those early years, he always came home.

Tumbledown raised a child who was not especially large, not particularly fearless, and seldom content with an explanation that did not answer the next question. It also gave him four people whose lessons would remain with him long after he took to the road.

## The ranger and the leatherworker's daughter

Ferris's mother, **Rowena Rumbuckle**, grew up in the workshop of her father, **Thaddius Thimblewood**, whom Ferris called **Pawpaw**. The Thimblewood family had made its living through leatherworking for generations. Thaddius expected his daughter to inherit both the work and the bench where he practiced it.

Rowena learned the trade. Then she chose the woods.

Her talent for finding paths and navigating the wilderness carried her into the service of Tumbledown's rangers. There she met **Graham Rumbuckle**, a ranger who had come to the village from beyond its familiar woods and shared her curiosity about the wild. They married, and Ferris was born into a family whose two callings—making things that lasted and venturing into dangerous country—would shape him in different ways.

Rowena taught her son to read the forest. A bent stem, a shallow impression, or a trail lost beneath leaves might tell him what had passed through and how recently. She taught him to wait, to move quietly, to distinguish one sign from another, and to keep looking when the path seemed to disappear.

Graham taught him what to do when following a trail brought him face to face with danger. His son could not rely on size, so Graham taught him position, timing, retreat, and what to do when escape was no longer possible. Eventually, he placed daggers in Ferris's hands and showed him how to use them.

## A home made by four teachers

Ferris's grandmother, known to him as **Grams**, taught another sort of survival. She knew which plants nourished and which made travelers ill; where to find water; how to build a fire; and how to turn an uncomfortable stretch of wilderness into somewhere people could rest. More than that, she taught Ferris to care for the companions who might have to share such a place with him.

Pawpaw taught him leatherworking. At the workbench, a rough hide could become something useful through cutting, punching, stitching, and fitting. Ferris enjoyed the small certainty of the work: a seam could be inspected, a mistake corrected, a worn piece strengthened. The process rewarded patience, steady hands, and close attention.

He could track. He could hunt. He could handle a knife, read a trail, and keep himself alive outside the village. **Leatherworking was what made him happy.**

There was a time when Ferris might have spent his life at the bench beside his grandfather. The woods would still have held mysteries, but he might always have had a warm workshop to return to when curiosity had taken him far enough.

## The rangers who did not return

When Ferris was **eleven**, something dangerous was sighted in the forest near Tumbledown. The village's rangers set out to stop it before it could reach the settlement. **Rowena and Graham** went with them.

Ferris had watched such departures before. His mother took her longbow, his father his daggers, and the rangers went where the village needed them. They had always come home.

This time the survivors returned without them.

Ferris remembered **Sylvie**, the lead ranger, appearing along the path, bloodied and accompanied by the equally battered leaders of the scouts and archers. Grams looked up from sweeping the workshop and let her broom fall. Pawpaw rose from his bench and hurried toward the door. The adults spoke in low voices, glancing at the boy as a blanketed bundle was placed before him.

Sylvie put a hand upon his shoulder and told him she was sorry.

**Rowena's longbow came back broken. Graham's daggers came back without him. Neither parent returned.**

The villagers told Ferris that Rowena and Graham had died protecting Tumbledown. They spoke of courage and sacrifice, of people spared because the rangers stood between them and the danger in the woods.

Ferris understood what the words meant. He could not understand why anyone believed they should make him feel better.

## The question nobody could answer

For a boy of eleven, the village was not the only thing his parents had been responsible for protecting. They had **him**. Ferris had been told that surviving danger sometimes meant retreating from it. He had been taught to follow a trail until he knew what lay ahead.

Why, then, had Rowena and Graham not run? Why had they stayed? Why had they not come home to their son?

He was angry at the creatures in the forest and at the adults who offered consoling praise. He was angry at every suggestion that he ought to feel proud. Sometimes he was angry at his parents themselves, a private anger he could neither explain away nor easily admit.

Pawpaw and Grams did not attempt to replace Rowena and Graham. They kept caring for Ferris as they always had. Grams fed and comforted him, made room for his grief, and kept their home from becoming only an empty place. Pawpaw kept a space open at the workbench.

When words brought no comfort, leather could still be cut and stitched. Ferris could make something with his hands. The work did not solve what had happened, but it offered a task whose next step he understood.

## A bow that could fire again

For years Rowena's broken bow and Graham's daggers remained among the possessions the family had kept. They were reminders of what had come back from the woods when Ferris's parents had not.

As Ferris grew older, he returned to the skills they had taught him. He still followed tracks and went farther into the forest than Grams would have liked. He learned to carry Graham's daggers in his own hands. Their fit was different now, but their purpose was familiar.

The bow required something more.

**Ferris repaired Rowena's longbow.** The work drew upon what he had learned at his grandfather's bench: patience, care, and respect for the material beneath his hands. He did not try to make the bow appear new. Its old wear, familiar marks, and traces of damage remained part of the object that had belonged to his mother.

He made sure it could fire again.

The weapon was not a replacement for Rowena, nor a promise that Ferris would follow precisely in her footsteps. It was something he could carry. When he took up the repaired bow and his father's daggers, the work of all four people who raised him traveled with him: **Rowena taught him to find; Graham to survive; Pawpaw to make and mend; Grams to live and care for others.**

## The rider at Tumbledown

Six years after his parents' deaths, when Ferris was **seventeen**, a terrible danger reached Tumbledown.

A **headless rider** came upon a nightmarish horse, wielding an **ebony blade wrapped in unnatural green flame**. Its arrival brought the violence the village's rangers had spent years trying to keep beyond the woods.

Ferris was no longer the child who waited for others to return. He could track, hunt, shoot, and fight; he knew how to survive alone in the forest. Yet none of those abilities guaranteed he could stop the thing before him.

**Pawpaw and Grams were in its path.** Both were badly injured. Others whom Ferris had known throughout his life fell amid the attack. The rider was larger, faster, and stronger than the young gnome, and Ferris felt the terrible inadequacy of every effort he made against it.

He was frightened in a way that his childhood explorations had never prepared him for. This was not a mysterious track he could abandon if the danger grew too great. There were people behind him who could not simply escape.

## The answer behind him

For six years Ferris had carried the question of why his parents had not come home. During the attack on Tumbledown, with Pawpaw and Grams endangered behind him, he began to understand a possible answer.

Rowena and Graham had not gone into the woods because they had forgotten their son. They had stood where danger would have to pass them **because Ferris was behind them**.

The realization did not erase his childhood grief, nor prove that every choice made by the village's rangers had been the only possible one. It did not bring his parents back. It changed what he understood of their courage.

Ferris had thought they chose the village instead of him. Now, standing between a terrifying enemy and the people he loved, he understood how they might have chosen to protect the place where their son lived.

He fought while afraid. He did not suddenly become stronger than the rider. He did not cease to feel small or helpless. But for the first time, he knew why a frightened person might stand their ground when those behind them depended upon it.

**Tumbledown survived the attack. So did Pawpaw and Grams, though barely. Ferris survived too.** The rider's ultimate fate was another matter.

## The leather that held

After the attack, Ferris carried a different kind of uncertainty. He understood something of his parents' choice, yet feared he had failed the village when it needed him. He remembered people falling and wondered whether Rowena or Graham would have done better.

He trained and resolved to leave Tumbledown **to hunt the headless rider**.

Pawpaw objected. He had already watched his daughter abandon the expected life of the workshop for the dangers of ranger service. He had already buried her. Now the grandson he had helped raise—stubborn, fearful, curious, and determined—meant to follow the same dangerous path.

They argued. Ferris chose to go.

Thaddius knew that anger would not turn him back. So he did what he could. If Ferris was going into a wilderness that had already taken so much from their family, his grandfather would make certain the leather protecting him was well made.

Ferris left with the equipment that joined his family's lives: his mother's restored bow, his father's daggers, and **the leather Pawpaw had made for him**.

It was not an assurance that he would come home. It was the care of someone who desperately wanted him to.

## The hunter who went anyway

Ferris is counted among the **[[Hunters Guild]]**, a fellowship whose work often carries its members into dangerous places beyond reliable help. His story begins long before any guild commission: at a workshop bench, among half-understood forest signs, and in a village where curiosity and fear grew together.

He is not remembered here as a hunter who conquered fear once and for all. The frightened boy remained part of the person who chose the woods. His curiosity could still lead him into trouble; his survival skills, leatherworking, and inherited weapons could help him meet it. His pursuit of the rider remained unfinished in the account of his departure.

The guild's old lesson—that protecting others asks more than strength—had already become a personal truth for Ferris. He had watched the rangers go out and fail to return. Years later he had stood in their place and understood, at last, why people with everything to lose might refuse to run.

His grandfather's farewell was not an oath, a blessing, or a promise of victory.

It was a piece of leather made carefully enough to withstand the road.
