## The comfortable story

For centuries, Avalor's children were taught that **Aldus Greystone III** had tried to save the realm from dangerous magic and that [[Jaznaar]], the drow among [[The Six]], had betrayed him at the [[Black Tower]].

The account reversed what had happened. Aldus was responsible for the forbidden experiments and their corruption. Jaznaar had worked to oppose and contain that danger.

The falsehood was not simply the invention of an inaccurate bard. The later inquiry established that **Cid’s agents deliberately circulated two connected legends**: Aldus the tragic savior and Jaznaar the treacherous saboteur.

## Why eyewitness memory did not protect the truth

The founders who confronted Aldus **did not believe Jaznaar had betrayed them**. Rynhold Frozenroar also knew him to be innocent, although he did not know that Aldus survived as Cid.

The accusation became dominant in **later generations**. As direct testimony passed from living memory, institutions copied and repeated the false account. Royal histories, songs, and religious records came to reinforce one another.

Their repetition gave the lie authority without making it true. Some historians rejected contradictions because they seemed less reliable than a familiar explanation; others preserved texts whose underlying testimony they could no longer test.

## From one accusation to the suspicion of a people

Some retellings treated an alleged crime by a single drow as evidence of a danger shared by all drow communities. The legend exploited prejudices already present in Avalor and strengthened them.

Under **Arnaud Frozenroar**, that suspicion became an excuse for [[arnauds-edicts|royal restrictions]], searches, and displacement. The king and those who enforced his policies remain responsible for their choices; a false legend did not remove their agency.

## The correction of the story

Following the defeat of Cid by [[The Misfits]], the recovered evidence overturned the old account. [[jaznaars-vindication|King Gradvum's proclamation]] publicly restored Jaznaar's name.

The [[royal-commission-historical-inquiry|Royal Commission]] preserves the false account as evidence of what earlier institutions taught—not because its accusation remains an equally credible interpretation.

Jaznaar's innocence has been established. What remains uncertain is how every generation will reckon with the consequences of having believed otherwise.
