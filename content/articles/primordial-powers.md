## Before the names of kings

The beginning of **Avalor** can be dated to the founding of a kingdom. The beginning of the world cannot.

Long before Rynhold Frozenroar raised a lion banner, people told stories about the arrival of light, the ordering of the world, and the powers that existed before anything recognizable as a kingdom or civilization. Some accounts speak of creation from nothing; others describe shape given to things that already existed. Their differences have survived even where the names of the first tellers have not.

In the oldest cosmological discussions, four names recur: **the Creator**, **the Formless One**, **Her**, and **the One Below**. They belong to Avalor's most ancient traditions, but they are not four members of an ordinary divine court.

The Creator is familiar from popular accounts of the world's beginning. The other three are far less widely known, appearing chiefly in obscure theological traditions and the work of scholars who study them. Later writers have arranged the four into a family, a ruling council, or a struggle of opposing forces. Such arrangements tell us more about the desire for an orderly explanation than about the fragmentary traditions from which the names survive.

## Four names, not one pantheon

The expression **primordial powers** gathers these names because of their antiquity, not because they are established as members of a single species or order of gods.

Even their titles resist neat comparison. The Creator appears to be named for an act. The Formless One for the absence of some defining shape or condition. Her for a relationship to change whose earliest meaning is difficult to recover. The One Below for a position, although the texts do not reliably explain what lies above it.

**[[Brother Halden Korr]]**, archivist of Heaven's Heart, sees the names as fragments of a lost understanding of existence. **[[Professor Xiomara Thal]]** cautions that ancient references need not have arisen from one shared doctrine. Traditions originally concerned with different beings might have been joined only by later commentators. **[[Lady Miren Dast]]** preserves the disagreement rather than selecting the more elegant picture.

The argument matters. If the four names once belonged together, their relationship may illuminate the earliest questions of creation. If they were joined afterward, a diagram placing them side by side could conceal precisely the differences that make their histories worth studying.

## The Creator and the first ordering

Of the four, **the Creator** is the best remembered. Across Avalorian accounts of beginnings, the title appears wherever people ask how existence came to have a recognizable shape or order.

Halden interprets the Creator as the original authority through which an ordered world became possible. To him, that conviction supports the idea that deeds and duties possess meaning because existence is not without moral structure.

Yet an ancient title does not explain an ancient deed. Did the Creator bring existence into being? Give form to what was already present? Establish principles through which a world could develop?

These are not identical acts, and the oldest stories do not reliably distinguish them. Thal asks what can safely be concluded from a title whose meaning has passed through countless generations. The name may preserve genuine devotion and still leave the actual process of creation unknown.

The earliest stories do not agree about **what was created, how it came into being, or whether something existed before it**.

## The Formless One and the question of identity

**The Formless One** presents a different problem. To lack form might mean having no physical body. It might mean possessing no single identity or unchanging nature. It might even describe a presence that refuses all the categories by which mortals recognize beings.

For that reason, some writers have associated the Formless One with possibility or the unknown. Such comparisons remain interpretations, not witnessed explanations.

The oldest references connect the Formless One to the Creator as an **ancient companion**. Their association does not readily resemble the picture of a lesser servant waiting upon a supreme ruler. Nor does the word *companion* prove equality of power, common purpose, or participation in the same act.

The two are remembered together, yet no surviving account reliably assigns them separate tasks in creation. Some believe the Formless One helped; others imagine a witness to the first ordering. It is also possible that their companionship concerned a history not preserved in the remaining fragments.

The name has prompted comparisons to the **Beyond**, but resemblance of language is not evidence that the Formless One and the Beyond are the same thing.

## Her and the work of becoming

Where the Creator invites questions about a beginning, **Her** is associated with **becoming**: the passage from one condition into another.

A seed becomes a tree. The tree ages and falls. Its decay feeds the soil from which another life may grow. To see only birth in that sequence would be to misunderstand it; change also includes loss, dissolution, and the making of something unrecognizable from what came before.

The idea extends beyond trees and seasons. A child matures. A craft develops. A settlement grows into a city. A kingdom changes as its people inherit, challenge, and rebuild the institutions left to them.

The old texts do not consistently treat growth and destruction as opposite divine functions. Both can be understood as transformations. For this reason, scholars sometimes compare traditions of Her to those of nature, renewal, and dawn, including the work of **[[Kelsalor]]** and devotion to **[[Lathander and the Devotion of the Dawn|Lathander]]**. Those comparisons do not make Her another name for those gods.

Some commentators understand Her as a personal presence; others emphasize a principle inseparable from the process of change. The oldest accounts leave that question open.

## The stone and the seed

Some druidic teaching circles influenced by [[Kelsalor]] place a **weathered stone beside a living seed** when discussing endurance and transformation. The stone seems to speak of permanence, the seed of change. Yet the stone wears away, and the seed depends on a world in which many older things have broken down and become soil.

The lesson, known as **the Stone and the Seed**, is not proof of a primordial intervention. It is an invitation to look more closely at what the words *lasting* and *changing* actually mean.

[[Heureux]] once joked that students could spend an hour arguing over which of the two changed more. The humor fits the lesson: the oldest philosophical questions are sometimes most clearly heard when someone makes them part of an ordinary conversation.

## The Ringtree Clan and a living devotion

For **[[Heureux|Heureux, called H]]**, Her is not merely a disputed name in an ancient document.

H was raised among the **Ringtree Clan**, a firbolg people living in concealed canopy settlements in the **[[Forest of the Fey]]**. They preserved oral and written histories and believed that **Her** touched animals, music, invention, memory, growth, and decay.

Their reverence makes clear why *becoming* need not be confined to the natural world. A song changes when sung by another voice. An invention transforms an everyday task. A history retold can rescue something from forgetting—or, when dishonestly altered, threaten the people whose lives it describes.

H's father taught him to preserve the clan's memory. After the destruction of its library and his flight from home, H continued that duty through song. The Ringtree's belief in Her remained part of his understanding of art and storytelling as living forms of memory.

This is a **specific tradition of one people**, not evidence that every firbolg or every druid shares the same form of worship. It also need not settle every scholarly argument about Her to be deeply meaningful to those who honor her.

## The One Below and the things unseen

**The One Below** is remembered through images of depth, concealment, and things left undisturbed.

Such images have often inspired fear. Depth suggests burial, confinement, dark passages, and threats hidden from ordinary sight. Yet something beneath the earth may also be a foundation, an old storehouse, or a place where a treasure has been preserved from harm.

The earliest references do **not** establish the One Below as evil.

Nor is the meaning of *below* certain. It may refer to a physical place, to a relationship among presences, or to a condition for which later languages have no faithful word.

Some religious commentators have identified the One Below with infernal powers; others have imagined a domain beneath the known planes. Neither proposal is established by an encounter. Attempts to equate the One Below with the **Beyond** are similarly unsupported; the traditions should not be collapsed because both are mysterious.

The uncertainty is part of this name's history. Its ambiguity has allowed generations to project their deepest fears upon it.

## The gods who receive prayers

The **[[The Threefold Devotion|Threefold Devotion]]** honors Tyr, Helm, and Torm in the Great Cathedral and among the paladins of Heaven's Heart. Other Avalorians follow traditions devoted to Lathander, Corellon, Moradin, Eilistraee, and additional powers known within particular communities.

These practices have rites, responsibilities, and living communities. The four primordial names occupy a different place in the sources. No comparable, continuous institution devoted to all four together has been established.

An Avalorian might believe the Creator stood at the world's beginning while praying to Tyr for justice, without seeing any contradiction. Another might regard primordial stories as philosophy while remaining devout toward the gods honored by their family.

Whether the gods arose after the primordials, were brought into existence by one of them, or stand in some different relationship remains **undetermined**. No reliable chronology of creation places every divine being in a single hierarchy.

## Stories of creation and the peoples of Avalor

Communities have long preserved accounts of their first ancestors and the making of their homelands. Such stories belong to living traditions and deserve to be understood on their own terms. They are not, however, evidence that the Creator or any other primordial made each ancestry through a separate act.

The kingdom's own founding offers a useful distinction. **[[Rynhold Frozenroar]]** united territories whose people had lived and remembered their past long before he took the crown. The kingdom began in **1 AF**; its people did not.

Nor should a proposed origin in primordial myth be used to assign greater virtue to one ancestry or condemn another. The centuries during which [[Jaznaar]] was falsely named the Betrayer demonstrate the harm that can follow when an inherited story is taken for proof of a person's nature.

## What the scholars can preserve

The [[The Great Library of Aldus Greystone|Great Library]] preserves theological texts whose age is sometimes easier to verify than their claims. **[[Shamman’s Tower]]** studies magic and the planes, uncovering complexities that earlier scholars could scarcely describe. Neither institution has produced an eyewitness history of the beginning of existence.

The story of the **[[The Six]]** and the false condemnation of **[[Jaznaar]]** offers a warning here. A belief may be repeated for centuries, recorded in authentic manuscripts, and honored by sincere people—and still be wrong about its subject.

Some instructional manuscripts at the Great Library deliberately leave **an empty space before the first stage of a diagram of creation**. Called the **Empty Beginning**, the gap acknowledges that no living scholar can date or describe the first event with confidence.

Older accounts also mention **the Keeper**, associated with the First Dawn and the boundaries between worlds. The Commission treats those references separately from the traditions of the four primordial powers.

The scholars of the Royal Commission therefore preserve the four names without inventing a complete account of their acts. The Creator remains bound to questions of beginning; the Formless One to an ancient companionship and an elusive nature; Her to becoming; the One Below to concealed depths.

Their names survived the loss of whatever first stories surrounded them. Learning to distinguish what those stories say from what later generations wish them to say is itself an act of respect.
