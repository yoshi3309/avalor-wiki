## The sixth name

For most of Avalor's recorded history, **Jaznaar Mizzrorzza** was remembered with an accusation instead of an honor. The name of the drow spellcaster appeared in official histories, religious commentaries, and songs as that of **the Betrayer**—the supposed traitor whose actions caused the catastrophe at the Black Tower.

That judgment has been overturned. The public evidence now establishes that Jaznaar was innocent of the betrayal attributed to him. He was one of [[The Six]], a founding hero whose later service protected the kingdom at a cost few of its citizens could have imagined.

The correction does more than restore one biography. It asks how an entire kingdom learned to distrust a defender and why so many generations repeated a story for which the surviving eyewitnesses had offered no support.

## Before there was a Betrayer

Jaznaar belonged to the fellowship of [[Aldus Greystone III]], [[Lowkee]], [[Faelynn]], [[Petris]], and [[Kelsalor]]. Like the others, his life became associated with Avalor's earliest struggles and the years that followed its founding.

He was close to Rynhold Frozenroar and trusted by his companions. His command of arcane knowledge could answer threats that ordinary arms or authority could not. Powerful, independent, and sometimes secretive, Jaznaar understood forms of magic that few of his companions could match. Yet his greatest act of service would require endurance rather than spectacle.

**His precise magical class remains unresolved.** Some surviving accounts call him a wizard and others a sorcerer. The Royal Commission therefore identifies him as a *spellcaster* rather than presenting a later preference as established fact.

What matters to his early history is not the title attached to his magic. It is that his friends relied upon him, including the healer with whom his fate would become so closely entangled.

## Friendship with Aldus

Before Aldus became the architect of the Blood Sickness, he was celebrated as a man who could heal when others could not. Jaznaar knew that earlier companion, and his memory of the healer made the later discovery of cruelty especially difficult.

The first investigations into the limits of healing were not, by themselves, recognized as dangerous. Aldus's friends had reason to hope that his work might save lives. Only gradually did the research pass beyond healing into an ambition to end mortality altogether, inflicting suffering in pursuit of a future he considered necessary.

When the harm became undeniable, Jaznaar did not defend the experiments. He opposed them. But he continued to hope that the friend he remembered could be rescued from the path he had chosen.

This distinction became tragically important. Generations later, the willingness to seek a way to save Aldus would be recast as complicity with him.

## Five friends, five different answers

As the experiments worsened, the remaining members of the Six faced a question that had no painless answer.

[[Lowkee]] came to believe Aldus had to die if necessary to end the danger; [[Faelynn]] increasingly shared that judgment. [[Petris]], whose devotion to Lathander emphasized renewal, still hoped for redemption. [[Kelsalor]] tried to mediate. Jaznaar sought a way to stop the work **without killing the man who had begun it**.

Their disagreement was over the possibility of saving Aldus, not over whether his victims mattered. All five recognized the suffering. None endorsed the cruelty.

The conflict between those positions, and the friendship that made them so difficult, is part of [[The Sundering of the Six|the Sundering of the Six]].

## The Black Tower

Approximately a decade after Avalor's founding, Aldus attempted a ritual at the [[Black Tower]], within the [[Spine of the World]]. He sought not merely to extend individual lives but to alter the very conditions of mortality. The ritual touched the boundary between the mortal world and the Shadowfell.

The five traveled to stop him. The surviving historical accounts differ on the precise sequence of the confrontation. They agree that the ritual was interrupted, Aldus's mortal body was slain, and the worst of his intended catastrophe was prevented.

No record reliably identifies one person who struck a final blow. The survivors believed Aldus defeated. For Jaznaar, the death of a friend he had hoped to save was therefore a loss even within a victory that spared the kingdom.

The full consequences of the ritual were not yet known. The [[The Blood Sickness|Blood Sickness]] remained, and the great upheavals of the landscape would endure long after the companions left the tower.

## The guardian no one thanked

Only later did Jaznaar learn that Aldus had not truly perished. His old companion survived in the Shadowfell, transformed into the being later called [[Cid the Blood King]].

Jaznaar faced a terrible choice: disclose that the catastrophe had not ended, or attempt to contain its surviving danger himself. He chose to bear the responsibility. He **bound Cid within the Shadowfell** and worked to contain much of the arcane corruption that Aldus's experiments had created.

For almost two thousand years, that struggle remained largely unknown to those it protected. Rulers came and went; towns flourished; new generations learned the names of the founding heroes. The preservation of their ordinary lives depended in part upon a service they could neither see nor acknowledge.

He had chosen his task, but the choosing did not make the passing centuries less lonely. Every generation that forgot his name left him defending a kingdom less willing to remember why he had stayed.

## How a defender became the Betrayer

The old accusation did not begin with the living founding companions. **They knew Jaznaar was innocent.** Rynhold Frozenroar also knew he had not betrayed the Six, though the king did not know Aldus had survived as Cid.

The lie spread over later generations. Cid's agents deliberately encouraged two intertwined stories: **Aldus the tragic savior** and **Jaznaar the saboteur** who had ruined the healer's attempt to protect the realm.

Repeated by institutions, copied into historical accounts, and sung in familiar ballads, the claim slowly acquired the weight of tradition. The absence of surviving eyewitnesses made it easier to treat a repeated accusation as settled truth.

It was not a harmless mistake. The story exploited and strengthened suspicion toward drow communities. Under [[Arnaud’s Edicts]], that prejudice helped justify discriminatory policies. Later reforms under [[The Reforms of Queen Reina|Queen Reina]] began reversing such injustices, but laws could not undo every injury or private belief.

The making of the false account is preserved in [[The Betrayer’s Legend]], which distinguishes the verified history from the deliberate deception that displaced it.

## The refuge beyond ordinary knowledge

Near the end of Jaznaar's long watch, [[The Misfits]] reached his **hidden refuge**. Their search had taken them toward a passage associated with the tomb of [[Bradgem Glav]] at [[Heroes Hill]]. **Two stones**, one of them the ice-blue crystal inherited by [[Tel'Lora Ebbenflow]] from his mother, played a part in finding the way. Beyond that hidden path, the adventurers finally met the last living member of the Six and learned what had happened at the Black Tower.

Jaznaar identified Aldus and Cid as the same being and described the danger he had spent centuries containing. He also instructed [[Tel'Lora Ebbenflow]], the half-elven wizard among the Misfits, entrusting him with knowledge important to the companions' understanding. The precise lessons passed between Jaznaar and Tel'Lora remain theirs; what followed was a renewed understanding of the danger Cid posed.

Before the companions faced Cid, Jaznaar placed an extraordinary weapon in **[[Lavitz Shawe]]'s** hands: **Cid's Bane**, the sword once carried by his friend **[[lowkee|Lowkee]]**. After centuries of betrayal and false accusations, a blade from the founding fellowship would be carried into the final confrontation by a new generation of protectors.

The meeting gave the adventurers a foundation for their final struggle, but the Royal Commission's historical judgment rests upon the recovered evidence as well as Jaznaar's testimony.

## The homecoming he did not see

**Jaznaar died after the Misfits departed his refuge.** Later, the companions laid him to rest beside the friends of his founding generation in [[Renada]].

He did not live to hear the kingdom publicly acknowledge his innocence.

The Misfits subsequently defeated Cid in the Shadowfell. With the truth brought into the light, [[King Gradvum Frozenroar]] formally proclaimed Jaznaar innocent, restoring his name to the royal record as one of Avalor's defenders.

In [[Rynhold Park]], the old inscription honoring Aldus was replaced with an account that acknowledged the corrected history. The original plaque survived in the collections of [[The Great Library of Aldus Greystone]], evidence of a story the kingdom had once repeated with undeserved confidence.

The proclamation, the monument, and the revised histories constitute Jaznaar's public vindication. They cannot return the centuries in which he was condemned, or repay the people who suffered because that condemnation was made useful to prejudice.

## A name that now belongs among the Six

Jaznaar's story is often told as a tragedy of sacrifice, but its meaning does not end with suffering. His actions preserved a kingdom that had ceased to remember him justly. The friendships of the founding age endured in the private truth of what its heroes had believed about one another, even while the public record was corrupted.

He is no longer to be named merely as the man accused of betraying the Six. He was one of them. He opposed their fallen companion's cruelty, carried a danger others could not yet understand, and gave the later heroes the knowledge they needed to confront it.

The restoration of his name is an obligation upon those who inherit Avalor's history: to remember not only the deeds of celebrated defenders, but also how easily an innocent person's memory may be made to serve a comfortable lie.
