## The faiths beyond the capital

A visitor who knows Avalor only through the [[The Great Cathedral|Great Cathedral]] might mistake the kingdom's most influential religious institution for the measure of all its devotion. In reality, beliefs and observances vary among households, settlements, and peoples whose histories reach far beyond the walls of [[Renada]].

Some families maintain a shrine. Others preserve sacred customs through seasonal gatherings, remembered songs, shared work, or ceremonies that mark births and deaths. The same town may contain several traditions, and a single family may inherit more than one.

It would be an error to assign each ancestry a fixed faith. The customs described here are examples recorded among particular communities, not rules for every elf, dwarf, drow, or other Avalorian.

## Art and the devotion to Corellon

Among some elven communities associated with the [[Forest of the Fey]], **Corellon** is honored through art, prayer, and ceremonies connected with the shaping of beautiful things.

These practices coexist with older relationships to groves and places of remembrance. Not every gathering in a forest belongs to the same rite, nor is every elven household devoted to Corellon.

The variety of observance is part of the region's lived history, rather than a gap awaiting a single official account.

## Belief belongs to communities, not bloodlines

The names of familiar gods recur across different peoples. **Tyr, Helm, and Torm** are revered by families whose ancestors came from many regions. Devotion to **Lathander** has crossed boundaries of ancestry and profession, as the life of [[Petris]] illustrates.

At the same time, regional customs give familiar beliefs a local character. A blessing asked over a workshop tool, a prayer offered at a forest gathering, or a song honoring a long-departed neighbor can belong to the same wider religion without being practiced everywhere in the same way.

No person can be assigned a faith simply from an ancestry. **[[The Peoples of Avalor]]** preserves the historical reasons why this distinction matters.

## The Ringtree Clan and Her

In the concealed canopy settlements of the **[[The Ringtree Clan|Ringtree Clan]]**, in the [[Forest of the Fey]], preserving memory was understood as a sacred responsibility. Their spiritual language centered on **[[The Primordial Powers#her-and-the-work-of-becoming|Her]]**, associated with growth, decay, invention, music, and the becoming of one thing from another.

The bard **[[Heureux]]**, raised by the clan's leading historian, learned to carry those traditions in song. After the destruction of his people's library, his wandering performances became a way to protect stories that could otherwise have disappeared.

The Ringtree's devotion demonstrates how a spiritual tradition can survive through both written learning and oral memory. It should not be mistaken for the universal faith of all firbolgs.

## Moradin and the work of the hands

In some dwarven households, **Moradin** is invoked at the forge, at the dedication of a workshop, and in remembrance of the patient craft of earlier generations.

Skill, labor, and the achievements of ancestors may have deep religious meaning. Yet remembrance should not automatically be confused with the worship of one's ancestors, and the customs of one settlement cannot speak for every dwarven family.

The public historical record describes a tradition of reverence toward craft without claiming one prescribed set of prayers or rites for all its practitioners.

## Eilistraee, song, and freedom

Some drow communities preserve devotion to **Eilistraee** through song, shared gatherings, and teachings of freedom and self-determination.

These customs have been particularly vulnerable to distortion. For centuries, the false accusation against [[Jaznaar]] helped spread suspicion toward drow communities, and during the reign of Arnaud Frozenroar such prejudice found expression in discriminatory decrees.

Sources produced by hostile outsiders sometimes described unfamiliar practices as evidence of forbidden magic. Many communities experienced displacement or the loss of their records. The surviving testimony therefore deserves care: scholars should neither repeat old accusations nor replace them with imagined details that the evidence does not establish.

The later [[The Vindication of Jaznaar|vindication of Jaznaar]] changed Avalor's public understanding of that history, but it could not restore every lost custom or undo every injury.

## Remembering through names and song

Devotion and remembrance can become inseparable when a people have suffered persecution. Among some drow families displaced by Arnaud's decrees, **Books of Names** recorded lost relatives and last known homes. Their purpose was not a uniform religious ritual; they were acts of care for people whom official records often failed to protect.

The **Names Spoken** gatherings later kept those lives present through recitation and shared memory. Such traditions may accompany prayer or song without requiring every participating family to worship the same god.

Similarly, the Ringtree Clan preserved both books and oral history. Their practice reflects devotion to Her, but also the human—and firbolg—need to remember what an institution might suppress or forget.

## Difference without division

Corellon, Moradin, and Eilistraee are not the only deities honored beyond the cathedral. Devotion to Lathander and to Tyr, Helm, and Torm also crosses the boundaries between communities and ancestries.

The same principle extends to families whose customs have not been fully recorded by the royal archives. The Northern Desert Tribes, among others, maintain practices shaped by their own histories; the available public sources do not warrant imposing a detailed doctrine upon them.

Avalor's diversity cannot be adequately described as a collection of peoples, each assigned a single god. Its faiths are living inheritances, altered by migration, kinship, disagreement, and the long struggle to practice them freely.
