## Reports brought before the crown

**Katiri Tenderfoot** holds the office of **Silver Hand** on [[The Aegis]]. It is the kingdom's established office of **official intelligence**, supplying information and judgment to those entrusted with decisions of state.

A rumor may travel faster than an army and do nearly as much damage if acted upon without examination. Reliable intelligence involves more than obtaining a report. It requires understanding who supplied it, what they witnessed, and what conclusions can responsibly be drawn from it.

## Knowledge and judgment

The duties of the Silver Hand are distinct from the public-order responsibilities of [[Rastrin Diltir]], the Warden of Order. The offices may both be concerned with danger, but the gathering and assessment of information are not identical to the enforcement of law.

The Aegis depends upon such distinctions. A report may be troubling without constituting proof. A threat may be genuine without justifying every measure proposed in response.

The public record confirms Katiri's official position but does not disclose a named roster of agents, particular methods of collection, or private networks attached to the Silver Hand. Those matters cannot be supplied merely by the imagination of later commentators.

## Intelligence and the kingdom's history

Avalor has learned the danger of trusting stories that flatter authority. For centuries the false condemnation of [[Jaznaar]] passed through institutions that believed themselves to be safeguarding the truth.

The historian's task and the intelligence officer's work are not the same, but both depend upon the ability to distinguish evidence from repeated assertion.

Katiri serves a council in which information must be weighed against finance, justice, military necessity, and obligations to the realm's many communities.

## The necessary restraint

The Silver Hand's value lies not only in what it can learn but in its willingness to report uncertainty. A sovereign who receives only confident conclusions may have less useful knowledge than one who is given the limits of the record.

That principle is central to the importance of the office, whatever practices remain beyond the published histories.
