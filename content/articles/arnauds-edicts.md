## A centuries-old accusation given authority

Under **Arnaud Frozenroar**, king from **1259 to 1315 AF**, the false accusation against [[Jaznaar]] became more than a story repeated in histories and sermons. The king's advisers invoked the danger of forbidden magic to justify restrictions on drow communities.

The kingdom had known genuine magical catastrophes, including the [[blood-sickness|Blood Sickness]]. Yet Arnaud's policies treated **ancestry as suspicion**, without requiring evidence that an individual had committed an offense.

Officials could demand residence records, search homes and writings, restrict travel, and remove families from places where they had lived for generations.

## What enforcement meant

Historical complaints describe merchants losing licenses, apprentices denied training, and neighbors accused on rumor. Some officials enforced the decrees zealously; others delayed or evaded them. The differences in enforcement did not make the laws harmless. A central policy gave discriminatory treatment legal force.

Some later surviving provincial copies preserve the language of public safety used to justify those measures. The Royal Commission cannot assume that every district used identical words or procedures.

The records do not yield a verified total of the people displaced or deprived of livelihood.

## The sanctuary dispute

Families fleeing enforcement found protection at [[Heaven's Heart]], among the [[The Cathedral Paladins]]. Their leader **Jeyse**, a deva, refused to surrender innocent people solely because of their ancestry. **Cal’el**, an aasimar identified with Tyr, Helm, and Torm, publicly upheld that refusal.

The confrontation ended through negotiation and growing opposition. The families were not handed over merely for being drow, and the harshest measures were curtailed. **There was no general war between the crown and Heaven's Heart.**

The event is remembered in [[sanctuary-at-heavens-heart|the Sanctuary at Heaven’s Heart]].

## The long afterlife of policy

Records created to enforce the edicts sometimes survived longer than the measures themselves. A person whose property had been seized did not recover it automatically when a restriction ended.

Arnaud's successor [[reinas-reforms|Reina]] curtailed additional laws and sought equal legal treatment, **centuries before Jaznaar's eventual vindication**. The reforms were real but could not instantly erase the fear, loss, and prejudice fostered by the edicts.

Arnaud's reign remains a warning of what happens when the state mistakes inherited accusation for proof.
